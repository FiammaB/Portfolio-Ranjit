Executive Summary

A flawless operational & regulatory pipeline — engineered from London to Jamnagar under global broadcasting deadlines.

Arrow Media, a premier UK production company, partnered with National Geographic to execute a high-stakes, wildlife-centric documentary series in Jamnagar, India. Operating under strict global broadcasting deadlines, the production required simultaneous clearances from federal ministries, diplomatic channels, and environmental divisions. The Challenges

01
Strict Wildlife Mandates

Filming wildlife assets in India requires navigating overlapping, non-negotiable federal environmental laws. A single missed clearance would stall a multi-million dollar international schedule.

02
Sovereign-Level Bureaucracy

Operating at the intersection of international media and Indian federal ministries required absolute legal precision, high-level administrative handling, and diplomatic coordination across multiple jurisdictions.

03
Time-Critical Crew Mobilisation

Securing specialised journalist visas for a large British production crew within an unyielding pre-production window — with departure set for 15 January 2026 from London Heathrow.

04
The Double-Entry Visa Precedent

Arrow Media's shoot schedule spanned multiple trips to India. The crew required double-entry J-visas — but Indian Embassy policy categorically does not grant multiple-entry visas under the Journalist visa category. This was not a grey area or a technicality to be worked around; it was a firm institutional policy, making the crew's phased travel into India legally impossible by default.

05
AWBI Permission for Wild Animals — A Near-Impossible Clearance

Arrow Media's documentary required filming with wild animals at Vantara. The Animal Welfare Board of India (AWBI) does not issue filming permissions involving wild animals — this is a statutory position, not a procedural hurdle. No standard application pathway existed. Securing this clearance meant finding and arguing a legitimate legal route through a body that had no mechanism to say yes.

How It Was Done — Challenges 04 & 05

04
Convincing the Embassy & MHA to Set a New Precedent

The Indian Embassy does not issue multiple-entry visas under the Journalist category. Full stop. Getting them to say yes required making the case that had never been made before.

The solution was not to challenge the policy but to reframe the production's requirement within it. Multiple detailed shoot schedules were constructed and presented to the Embassy — demonstrating that the crew's return visits were not discretionary travel but contractually mandated, production-critical entries tied to distinct phases of a single commissioned project. This reframing gave the Embassy and the Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA) the grounds to justify an exception: double-entry J-visas were issued, a rare outcome that required persistent, evidence-based diplomatic engagement at the highest level of the consular process.

Double-Entry J-Visa — First of Its Kind MHA Approval Secured Multiple Shoot Schedules Filed Embassy Policy Exception
05
Unlocking a Permission That Did Not Exist

AWBI does not issue permissions for filming with wild animals. There is no form, no precedent, no standard process. The permission had to be built from nothing.

The strategy was to approach the clearance not as an AWBI application — which would have been rejected outright — but through a parallel multi-authority framework. By securing the MoEFCC Wildlife Division's NOC and the Central Zoo Authority's no-objection first, and then routing the wild animal filming permissions through the Chief Wildlife Warden of Gujarat under the Wildlife Protection Act, 1972, a legally airtight pathway was constructed that satisfied the intent of AWBI's mandate without triggering its standard exclusions. The result: Arrow Media's crew filmed with wild animals at Vantara — something that, by default, should not have been possible.
